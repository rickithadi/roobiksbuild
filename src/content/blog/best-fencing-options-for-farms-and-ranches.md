Farmers and ranchers need fencing that does one job well: keep livestock in and predators out, season after season, without a maintenance schedule that eats into the workday. The right choice depends less on style and more on what's actually walking the line — cattle, horses, and goats each push a fence differently, and the ground itself (rocky, sloped, wet) decides what will hold.

## Woven and barbed wire

Still the workhorse for large-acreage cattle operations. Woven wire is the safer choice around horses, since it won't catch a hoof the way barbed wire can. Barbed wire costs less per foot and goes up fast, which matters when you're fencing hundreds of acres rather than a yard.

## Electric fencing

For rotational grazing, nothing beats a well-grounded electric line. It's cheap to extend, easy to move as pasture sections rotate, and modern chargers hold a strong pulse even on long runs. The tradeoff is that it needs power and regular checking — a downed line an animal has already learned to ignore is worse than no fence at all.

## Wood post-and-rail

Where the fence line runs along a driveway, a pasture visible from the road, or an entrance a buyer will actually see, wood post-and-rail earns its keep. It reads as established and cared-for, and treated correctly it holds up for decades in most climates.

## Cattle panel and pipe

For working pens, loading chutes, and anywhere animals will test a fence under pressure, welded pipe and heavy cattle panel are worth the extra material cost. This is not the place to save money on lighter-gauge fencing.

**Our approach:** we walk the property, ask what's actually going to lean on the fence, and spec material by section rather than defaulting to one product for the whole perimeter. A ranch entrance and a back forty rarely need the same fence.
