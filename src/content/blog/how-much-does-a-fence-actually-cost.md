The most common question we get isn't "what material should I use" — it's "why did one quote come in at half the price of another." Usually the answer is scope, not markup: what's included, what condition the ground is in, and how many gates and corners the run actually has.

## What actually drives price per foot

Materials matter, but labor and site conditions often move the number more than the fence itself. Removing an old fence, digging through roots or rock, grading a slope, and pulling a permit all add cost before a single new post goes in the ground. Two quotes for "the same fence" can differ by thousands of dollars because one contractor walked the yard and one didn't.

## Chain-link

The lowest cost per foot for both material and labor, and still the right call for a lot of commercial and utility applications where appearance isn't the priority. Adding privacy slats or a vinyl coating raises the price but keeps it well below most alternatives.

## Wood

Mid-range, and the range itself is wide — a basic dog-ear picket fence and a board-on-board privacy fence with steel posts are both "wood," priced very differently. Wood also carries a real cost over time: staining or sealing every few years to protect the investment.

## Vinyl and composite

Higher upfront cost than wood, but close to zero maintenance after installation — no staining, no rot, no annual touch-up. For homeowners planning to stay put for a decade or more, the lifetime cost often comes out close to or below wood.

## Ornamental steel and aluminum

The highest cost per foot, justified by longevity (decades, not years) and the security and curb-appeal profile that comes with it. This is the material most likely to actually raise a home's resale value rather than just maintain it.

**Our approach:** every quote comes from an on-site walk, not a phone estimate — gate count, ground condition, and old-fence removal included up front, so the number you see is the number you pay.
