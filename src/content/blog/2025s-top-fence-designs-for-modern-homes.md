Fencing used to be an afterthought — something added once the landscaping was finished. That's flipped. Homeowners are treating the fence as part of the home's design language from the start, and the requests we're getting this year reflect it.

## Horizontal slat fencing

The single biggest shift we've seen: horizontal boards instead of vertical pickets. It reads as contemporary, makes a narrow yard feel wider, and pairs naturally with the clean lines of modern and mid-century homes. It costs a bit more in labor — the framing has to be more precise — but the payoff in curb appeal is real.

## Mixed materials

Wood-and-metal combinations are having a moment: steel posts and frames with horizontal wood infill, or a low masonry base topped with a slatted wood or steel screen. Mixing materials lets a fence carry more visual weight at the entrance while staying lighter and more open along the rest of the property line.

## Board-on-board privacy fencing

For yards that need real privacy without looking like a stockade, board-on-board alternates overlapping planks on each side of the rail, closing every sightline while still allowing airflow through the fence — better for the wood, and it doesn't trap heat against the boards the way a solid panel can.

## Minimal metal fencing

Thin-profile aluminum and steel pickets, often in matte black, are showing up on pool enclosures and front-yard boundaries where owners want a defined edge without blocking the view of the landscaping they just paid for.

**Our approach:** we ask what the fence needs to do — privacy, a pool code requirement, a defined front boundary — before we talk material, so the design serves the yard instead of the other way around.
